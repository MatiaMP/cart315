I used to believe prototypes were simply used as a tool to test builds. You build a prototype, test it out, have others test it out and see if there are any issues, anything that can be improved on. For example, in the context of a game prototype, if the gameplay is smooth, if the UI is working well, if there are any quality of life aspects that can be improved on, etc. The authors argue that this does not do prototypes justice, prototypes are also how designers think, which helps them expore a design space, discover new possibilities and refine ideas, not just proving them right or wrong.

What I found most useful is that prototypes usually work as filters. A prototype is valuable because it is incomplete. "It is the incompleteness that makes it possible to examine an idea's qualities without building a copy of the final design". The house case study proves this concept. People who saw the 2D blueprint spoke about layout and room relationships. While people who saw the 3D model, talked about atmosphere and how the space felt. They were prototypes about the same house yet people formed different understandings of it. Choosing what to filter is a decision in itself.

The second idea, about prototypes as manifestations, was also interesting. In the phone case study, only twenty percent of the usablity findings were common to all three prototypes. Some problems came from the prototype itself, such as users clicking the screen image because it looked like a computer interface, rather than from the design being test. I had assumed a prototype was a neutral window onto a design.

Although the article focuses on interaction design, I can use this as a practical habit. Before building a prototype I can ask myself what do I actually want to explore and how will my choices bias a user's feedback. Do I want to focus on the core mechanics of the game, level flow, UI, choice of materials? My choice of materials in particular could bias how playtesters respond so some of the feedback may be about the prototype itself rather than the game.
